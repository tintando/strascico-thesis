Prior corpora are snapshots: a scraper walks each channel once, prints done, and stops. Whatever changes afterwards is invisible. One way to capture these changes is Telegram's live update stream, which requires an account that joins every channel it watches and stays connected. One predecessor used this approach, Kireev and colleagues, they received new messages live from thirteen channels, albeit ignoring the edit and deletion notifications. To watch thousands of channels, you cannot join them all.

Our answer is the revisit machine. It visits public channels over and over, forever. Every visit fetches new messages, snapshots the channel's metadata, and walks into the linked discussion group where the comments live. What changed since the last visit is recovered by diffing, and how that stays affordable, I will show in a moment. The corpus grows on its own: when a post is forwarded from a channel we have never seen, the scraper follows the forward. Discovery snowballs, one forward at a time.

So how does a visit stay affordable? The corpus holds nearly half a billion messages. Re-reading everything on every visit would be very expensive: at Telegram's rate limits, a single pass would take months. So each visit opens with one cheap question instead: how many messages does this channel hold right now?

Telegram answers with the channel's total message count. We fetch the new messages first, then compare what we hold (including messages from previous visits) against what Telegram reports. If the two counts match, nothing was deleted anywhere in that channel. If the count is short, the gap says exactly how many vanished, and only then do we pay for a wide scan, walking backwards until each one is found. To catch edits, in the case of no deletions we re-fetch the last **N** messages, while in the other case we piggyback off the re-fetch needed to find the deleted messages. There is a trade-off: we lose exact timestamps and get detection time instead. In exchange, twelve thousand channels, not thirteen.

So what do the layers show? Start with edits. Telegram stamps an edit date on every edited message, and by that flag, one message in three has been edited. That is misleading. Of the edits we caught and diffed, ninety-three percent left the text byte for byte identical: it might be added media, link previews resolving, formatting. The flag over-counts real rewriting about ten times, and only the visit-to-visit diff, which holds both versions, can tell the difference.

Deleted content runs 1.62× more toxic than what survives. Choice targets toxicity; purges don't. 21:14
Deletions are a different story. Two point four million messages vanished under our watch. Comments die four times as often as posts, and most deleted comments go in bulk, when an entire thread is purged, for example as a result of a broadcast post being deleted. Deleted content is about one point six times more toxic than what survives. Individually deleted messages run far more toxic than those removed in thread purges. Selection targets toxicity; purges do not.

So, *three contributions*. A system: a scraper that recovers edits and deletions without staying connected, by revisiting and diffing. A corpus: nearly half a billion messages with three change layers, including a channel metadata snapshot at every visit. And a showcase: what we just saw. The system inherently compromises: detection intervals are more or less tight, snowball bias is difficult to correct, and corpus rates are not platform prevalence. And the future work writes itself, because the scraper is still running: sustained running turns samples into dynamics. The deletion sample becomes moderation dynamics, the edit archive becomes a history of corrections, and the comment side becomes audience dynamics: who comments, who returns, and how reactions shift over time. And that's it, Thank you.
